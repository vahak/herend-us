How to Read Herend Marks and Backstamps

Last updated October 24, 2026. Written by Tamar Medzadourian, Paris Tableware, Ridgewood, New Jersey.

Turn a piece of Herend over and the mark on the base tells you three things: the factory trademark, a number under it that identifies the shape, and a letter code that identifies the decoration.

The three parts of a Herend mark

  1. The trademark. The factory mark with the Herend name and the country. Many marks recorded from the 1930s onward add the words "hand painted." Some shoppers search for this wording as "Herend Hvngary hand painted"; compare the wording on your own piece with the dating table further down this page.
  2. The shape number. The number under the trademark names the shape. Every dinner plate of the same shape carries the same number, whatever the pattern.
  3. The decor code. Letters that name the decoration: the pattern on dinnerware, or the fishnet color on a figurine.

Decor codes: which letters mean which pattern

These codes come from the item numbers in our Herend catalog. The layout on a base can differ, so compare the letters and digits and ignore spacing and dashes.

Each figurine has its own five-digit model number instead, such as 15093 for the Herend Owl Figurine. In the item numbers we carry, the digits after the shape number separate parts and variations. On a cup and saucer, 00734-1 is the saucer and 00734-2 is the cup. The final pair marks a variation: Rothschild Bird dinner plates run from -0-01 to -0-12, one for each of the pattern's twelve motifs. On some porcelain boxes, the final pair changes with the figure on the lid.

Why the anniversary marks count from different years

According to Herend's history timeline, the 1939 centenary exhibition took 1839, the year Mór Fischer took over, as the founding year. Later research established 1826, when Vince Stingl founded the works, and the manufactory celebrated its 150th anniversary in 1976. So the 1939 and 1964 marks count from 1839, and the 1976 mark counts from 1826 (see our Herend history timeline). "Éves" is Hungarian for "years old."

Second-quality marks and the so-called student mark

The collector site theoldstuff.com says two cuts across a mark from 1948 onward mean second quality, and it pictures a "student mark" used on third-quality pieces painted by students. Treat this as a collector report, not a factory record.

Can a mark prove a piece is real?

A mark alone can't, because a backstamp can be copied. When you buy new Herend, the seller matters as much as the mark:

  • Buy from an established retailer with a street address, a phone number and a written return policy.
  • Be wary of a new piece offered far below its usual retail price.
  • When it arrives, check that the decor code on the base matches the pattern or color you ordered.

Before you buy a piece secondhand:

  • Ask for clear photos of the base as well as the front.
  • Check that the decor code fits the pattern name. A Rothschild Bird piece should show RO, and Chinese Bouquet Green should show AV.
  • Compare the shape number with a current piece of the same kind. A mismatch is worth a question.

These checks reduce risk but can't authenticate a piece.

Common questions

What does "Herend Hungary hand painted" on the bottom mean?

It is part of the Herend trademark. Collector records show the words "Herend Hungary Handpainted" on marks from the 1930s onward. Next to the trademark you will usually find a shape number and a letter code for the decoration.

How can I tell how old my Herend piece is?

Compare the mark with the eras collectors have recorded. Marks from 1939 to 1948 often include a year, and from 1990 many marks carry a date code: a month letter from A (January) to L (December) plus a two-digit year, so K91 is November 1991. Treat these as collector reports, not factory records.

Is there a Herend student mark?

The collector site theoldstuff.com pictures a mark it calls a student mark, used on third-quality pieces. Treat it as a collector report, not a factory record.

How do I know if my Herend is real?

A mark alone can't prove it, because a backstamp can be copied. Buy new pieces from an established retailer, check that the decor code and shape number fit the piece, and be wary of prices far below retail. For a secondhand piece, ask for clear photos of the base before you buy.
